Ecological art education · 2026

Rethinking Ecological Art Education through Plants as a Medium: A Practice-Based Study.

The Plant Cup

Learning with living plants through embodied observation, material experimentation, and collective making.

Abstract

As ecological crises intensify and sustainability becomes a shared global concern, ecological art education increasingly incorporates engagement with nature and society. Yet teaching practices can still objectify natural beings or rely on formulaic activities, reducing living organisms to visual references, symbols, or materials. Grounded in plant philosophy and the ontology of plant life, this study reviews existing approaches to ecological art education and proposes a teaching pathway that treats plants as a medium.

The study develops a model with four interconnected dimensions: pedagogical philosophy, teaching relationships, curriculum content, and practice modules. The curriculum moves through three themes: perceiving and responding to plant life signals; narrating and participating in growth processes; and observing and experiencing survival strategies. Perceptual guidance, embodied participation, and the co-creation of learning situations support sustained, multidimensional encounters with plants. The teaching practice suggests that making plants a central variable can shift learning from outcome-oriented production toward process-oriented participation, opening artistic practice to interspecies collaboration and greater sensitivity to ecological relationships.

This approach expands the possibilities of ecological art education. By attending to the agency, subjectivity, and temporality of plant life, it moves beyond human-centered observation and production toward cultural participation grounded in multispecies coexistence. It offers methodological insights for ecological art education within foundational design and contemporary art curricula, while extending the role of art education in ecological ethics and cultural transformation.

Keywords: Ecological art education; plants as a medium; teaching practice; human–nature relationships; ecological awareness

01 / Research questions

What changes when plants shape the learning process?

The inquiry addresses the tendency to reduce nature to visual references or materials, and the limits of predetermined teaching activities.

  1. How can plant signals, growth, and adaptation inform the design of ecological art education?
  2. How might encounters with plants reshape students’ observation, participation, and artistic decisions?
  3. What possibilities and limitations emerge when living processes introduce uncertainty into teaching?

Research logic

How the inquiry develops

The study moves from a critique of existing teaching approaches to a proposed framework, teaching practice, and reflective artistic translation.

Theoretical foundation

Plant sensing and agency

Plant philosophy invites us to approach plants as living beings with their own ways of inhabiting the world, extending ethical attention beyond their usefulness to humans (Marder, 2013). Research on plant intelligence examines how plants sense environmental conditions and coordinate adaptive growth; Trewavas (2003) interprets these capacities in terms of adaptive problem-solving, while Mancuso (2018) emphasizes their distributed, modular organization.

For this project, these ideas inform three perspectives: structural agency—growth and regeneration through a modular body; sensory agency—responses to light, gravity, moisture, and touch; and behavioral agency—adjustments in growth and resource allocation. These perspectives guide activities in which students observe plant signals, follow growth over time, and respond through artistic practice. Here, agency describes responsiveness and adaptation; it does not presume human-like subjective consciousness, for which claims remain contested (Taiz et al., 2019).

References

  • Marder, M. (2013). Plant-Thinking: A Philosophy of Vegetal Life. Columbia University Press.
  • Trewavas, A. (2003). Aspects of plant intelligence. Annals of Botany, 92(1), 1–20.
  • Mancuso, S. (2018). The Revolutionary Genius of Plants: A New Understanding of Plant Intelligence and Behavior. Atria Books.
  • Taiz, L., Alkon, D., Draguhn, A., Murphy, A., Blatt, M., Hawes, C., Thiel, G., & Robinson, D. G. (2019). Plants neither possess nor require consciousness. Trends in Plant Science, 24(8), 677–687.

Conceptual shift

From observing plants to learning with them

The research examines limitations that can arise when nature is treated primarily as a visual reference. It proposes extending teaching toward living processes and reciprocal encounters.

02 / Pedagogical framework

Reconfiguring the teaching relationship

Teaching shifts from observation-led instruction toward a dynamic learning community in which teachers, students, plants, and the surrounding natural environment shape the learning process together.

An interconnected teaching model

The spherical structure brings teaching principles, curriculum content, and practice modules into one relational model. Its connections indicate opportunities for interaction rather than a prescribed sequence of lessons.

Perception-oriented practice

Attend to plant characteristics through sensory observation and artistic response.

Action-oriented practice

Participate in material and environmental processes through situated making.

Situation-oriented practice

Explore ecological relationships through imagined environmental scenarios.

03 / Research methods

Design, observe, reflect, revise

The study combines theoretical inquiry with action research. Teaching practice provides a setting for examining the feasibility of the proposed approach and identifying changes needed in subsequent teaching.

Literature review

Ecological art education, environmental aesthetics, and plant philosophy establish the conceptual basis for the teaching model.

Action research

Teaching modules are developed and enacted, then examined through student feedback, teaching logs, and reflection.

Case studies

Individual and group work is considered alongside teaching records and interviews to examine perception, creative decisions, and ecological understanding.

Artistic translation

The researcher’s creative practice translates pedagogical ideas and teaching outcomes into forms that can be encountered in an exhibition.

05 / Research reflections

What the practice made visible

Attention to process

Teaching observations suggest that students attended more closely to plant processes and environmental relationships. These observations suggest possibilities for process-oriented teaching; they do not establish a measured causal effect.

Uncertainty as a teaching condition

Plant growth and environmental change do not follow a classroom schedule. The research identifies a need for flexible activities and longer-term observation.

The limits of interpretation

Students may attribute human emotions or intentions to plants. Reflection therefore includes distinguishing artistic speculation from claims about plant experience.

Directions for further work

Longer teaching cycles, stronger ecological knowledge, and clearer evaluation procedures could help examine how these experiences develop over time.

06 / From research to exhibition

The Plant Cup

The Plant Cup translates the broader teaching inquiry into an exhibition. Moving images document the teaching process, while a circular text display and white sculptural forms bring speculative plant futures into a shared space.

Student-generated scenarios and designs inform the work. The installation is the researcher’s artistic response to this collective teaching process.
